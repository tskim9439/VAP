\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1_task.pdf}
  \caption{\task on one mono stream (invented utterances). The system hears only the mix and commits, without retraction, translations into each listener's language and, optionally, a speaker-tagged transcript (Output 1). Ticks: committed pieces; the shaded band is a turn-end overlap (condition L1, \cref{sec:bench}). \eo: from a turn's end to its last translated word; switch latency: from the start of a turn in a new direction to its first translated word.}
  \label{fig:task}
\end{figure*}

Two people who share no language can talk through a device that translates both sides, a goal of early speech translation systems \citep{waibel1996interactive,wahlster2000verbmobil} that deployed services now offer \citep{lewis2015skype}.
The device stands in for a dialogue interpreter, who coordinates turns as well as translating them \citep{wadensjo1998interpreting,roy2000interpreting}.
Simultaneous speech translation (SimulST) has scaled to large streaming models \citep{seamless2023seamless,labiausse2025highfidelity}, which IWSLT evaluates on long unsegmented audio \citep{abdulmumin2025findings,adelani2026speech}.
Yet most work uses human pre-segmented speech \citep{papi2025how}, and SimulST benchmarks translate one source language in one fixed direction, mostly from one speaker.

Conversation changes the problem in four ways (\cref{fig:task}).
(i)~\emph{The direction is latent}: the system must segment one mono stream at speaker changes and route each turn to its direction; with one language per speaker, routing is language identification, but changes must be detected within gaps of a few hundred milliseconds or during overlap.
(ii)~\emph{Turns are short and close}: the median silence-trimmed turn in our real dialogues lasts 2.86\,s, and in natural conversation replies follow gaps of about 200\,ms \citep{levinson2015timing} or overlap, as about 40\% of speaker changes do \citep{heldner2010pauses}.
(iii)~\emph{Output is committed}: a listener may reply once text appears, and no revision undoes that reply, so we score append-only output; re-translation systems \citep{arivazhagan2020retranslation} take part through a commit rule (\cref{sec:exp-setup}).
(iv)~\emph{Latency is felt at turn ends}: listeners wait after each turn (\eo) and at each change of direction (switch latency), beyond the within-turn lag that \laal measures \citep{papi2024streamatt}.
We therefore pose \taskfull (\task; \cref{sec:task}) as streaming structured prediction from a mixture, with a latent speaker and direction state and append-only, time-stamped translations whose language encodes the direction, and score it under realistic and oracle segmentation.
Interruptions, barge-in, backchannels, more speakers, code-switching and speech output are out of scope.

Among the closest systems (\cref{tab:positioning}), JSTAR \citep{moritz2025transcribing} jointly streams recognition and translation of bilingual conversations and infers the speaker, hence the direction, but from spatial cues of multi-microphone smart glasses, on in-house data.
DiariST \citep{yang2024diarist} and STAC-ST \citep{zuluagagomez2023end} attribute speakers in one channel but translate from one source language, STAC-ST only offline.
Seed LiveInterpret~2.0 \citep{cheng2025seed} names multi-speaker confusion as a challenge, but this closed system is evaluated per direction without overlap and only for Chinese--English, absent from \bench, so we do not run it.
Neither single-channel input nor conversational translation is new, but to our knowledge no public benchmark, open baseline or model addresses streaming translation of both sides of a two-language conversation from one mono stream, where turns and their directions are latent and may overlap.

We ask how much of the difficulty lies in segmentation and routing rather than translation (\textbf{RQ1}), how quality, lag and listener wait trade off as turn-taking tightens and for a distant language pair (\textbf{RQ2}), whether one streaming model closes the oracle--realistic segmentation gap without oracle input (\textbf{RQ3}), and whether synthetic sessions rank systems as real ones do (\textbf{RQ4}).
